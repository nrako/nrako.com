A prototype used to cost two weeks, so showing one meant somebody had already
argued for it. Now it costs an afternoon, and the meeting where it is shown
has quietly become the meeting where it is approved.

The room sees something that works. Nobody wants to be the person who asks
what it is for.

## What a demo proves

It proves the thing can exist. It says nothing about the three questions that
decide whether it should:

1. Who stops doing something else in order to use it?
2. What do we maintain for the next five years because of it?
3. What did we not build this quarter instead?

A demo answers "can we?" with such confidence that "should we?" feels rude.

## Keeping the two apart

I ask for the demo and the decision on different days. The first meeting ends
with applause and no commitment. The second starts with a page of text and no
screen.

It sounds like ceremony. In practice it is the only way I have found to let a
good idea be *declined* without anyone feeling that their afternoon was
wasted.
